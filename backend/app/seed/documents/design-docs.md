# Writing design docs people actually read

A design doc is a tool for thinking, and only then a tool for communicating. Most of the value comes from the writing: the gaps in a plan become obvious the moment you try to explain it in full sentences.

## Start with the problem

Spend the first paragraph on the problem and why it matters now. Reviewers who agree on the problem will forgive a lot in the solution; reviewers who do not agree on the problem will argue about every detail of it.

## Write down what you are not doing

A short list of non-goals saves more review time than any diagram. It tells readers which objections are out of scope before they write them.

## Alternatives considered

Every serious design has at least two alternatives that were rejected. Write them down with the reason they lost. Future readers will ask "why didn't you just..." and this section answers them without a meeting.

## Keep it short

If the doc is longer than six pages, split it or cut it. Put the details in an appendix. A design doc that nobody finishes reading has not been reviewed, no matter how many approvals it collected.

## After the review

Update the doc when the design changes during implementation, or mark it clearly as outdated. A wrong design doc is worse than none at all.
