# Reading research papers in three passes

I used to read papers the way I read novels: from the first word to the last, hoping understanding would arrive somewhere along the way. It usually did not. These are the notes I wish I had when I started.

## First pass: five minutes

Read the title, the abstract and the introduction, then skim the section headings and the conclusion. Ignore the maths. At the end of the first pass you should be able to say what kind of paper it is, what problem it claims to solve and whether it is worth another twenty minutes.

Most papers stop here, and that is fine. The first pass is a filter, not a failure.

## Second pass: an hour

Read the paper properly but skip proofs and anything you would need to look up. Pay attention to the figures: a good figure carries most of the argument, and a bad one is often where the weak spots hide. Write down the terms you did not understand and the references you want to follow.

The goal of the second pass is to be able to summarise the main thrust of the paper, with supporting evidence, to someone else.

## Third pass: re-implement it in your head

The third pass is for papers you need to really know: ones you are reviewing, building on, or arguing against. Try to recreate the work from scratch, making the same assumptions as the authors, and compare your version with theirs. This is where you find the hidden assumptions and the missing citations.

## Keeping notes

Highlights alone are not notes. Every time I highlight a passage I try to write one sentence in my own words next to it, saying why it matters. If I cannot write that sentence, the highlight was probably a reflex rather than a thought.

Tag notes by question, not by paper. "How do they evaluate?" is a much more useful tag a year later than the name of a conference.
